# Week 6 Reflection

CNNs build up features from edges and textures to full objects. How did this idea of progressive feature extraction influence the way you thought about refining your BBO strategy?
The idea of progressive feature extraction made me think about my surrogate differently. In a shallow network, each hidden layer can only capture linear combinations of inputs, much like a CNN edge detector that has no concept of shape yet. With two hidden layers, the second layer can in principle combine the patterns found by the first into something more abstract, such as the interaction between two input dimensions rather than their individual effects. For F5 and F8, where higher dimensions seem to interact to produce large outputs, I kept the two-layer architecture for this reason rather than flattening it to a single layer. The parallel is imperfect at 15 data points, but it shaped how I thought about whether reducing the model would actually help or whether it would simply lose the capacity to detect structure that might be there.

LeNet and later CNNs redefined what is possible in computer vision. What parallels do you see between those breakthroughs and the incremental improvements you make in your BBO capstone project?
The most relevant parallel for me is not the size of the breakthrough but the mechanism behind it. LeNet succeeded because it encoded prior knowledge about images directly into the architecture: locality, translation invariance, spatial hierarchy. Each of those was a deliberate structural choice, not just a larger network. My incremental improvements follow a similar logic. Moving from the fixed blend heuristic in rounds 1 and 2, to the SVM classifier in round 3, to the neural network surrogate from round 4 onwards, each step encoded more prior knowledge about the problem: that output is a continuous function of inputs, that dimension sensitivity varies across functions, that exploration bonus terms prevent the strategy from collapsing to a single region. The scale is entirely different, but the principle of encoding structure rather than searching blindly is the same.

Training CNNs often involves balancing depth, computational costs and overfitting risks. Did you face similar trade-offs when choosing whether to explore widely or exploit promising regions in your queries?
Yes, and round 6 illustrated it clearly. For F4, the query sits only 0.032 units from the nearest previously observed point, which suggests the surrogate was confident enough in a known region to exploit it closely. For F8, the query sits 0.628 units away, suggesting a much more exploratory choice. Both decisions came from the same algorithm, but the surrogate landscapes were different: F4 has high cross-validation error that made me less confident in distant predictions, while F8 has lower error and more spread-out training points that pointed toward a genuinely new region. The analogy to depth versus overfitting is that a deeper model fitted on too little data overfits and produces unreliable predictions away from training points, exactly the condition that pushes my acquisition function toward nearby exploitation rather than distant exploration.

Convolutions, pooling, activations and loss functions influence how CNNs learn from data. Which of these concepts helped you think differently about how your optimisation model learns from your accumulated data?
The activation function was the most useful of these concepts for me this round. I use tanh activation throughout. In a CNN, ReLU is typically preferred because it avoids vanishing gradients over many layers. For my two-layer surrogate applied to a bounded input domain, tanh behaved better because it saturates smoothly near the boundaries of the input space rather than cutting off at zero. This mattered for gradient ascent: when refining a candidate query by following the numerical gradient of the surrogate, a ReLU network near a boundary can produce flat zero-gradient regions that stop the refinement early, whereas tanh continues to provide gradient signal even close to 0.0 and 0.999999. The loss function concept was also clarifying: minimising mean squared error on a normalised output scale is equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation under Gaussian noise, which made it easier to think about what the surrogate was actually assuming about the functions.

The interview with Andrea Dunbar highlighted the trade-offs of deploying CNNs in edge AI systems. How might reflecting on real-world deployment challenges help you decide how to benchmark success in your own BBO capstone project?
Dunbar's point about edge deployment was that the right performance metric depends entirely on the constraint you are optimising under: latency, power, accuracy, or model size. In my capstone, the equivalent question is what counts as success given the constraints I actually face. The constraint is not compute or latency but query budget: I get one query per function per round and cannot recover a wasted one. By that measure, success is not a low surrogate error metric, it is whether the returned output is higher than the previous best. F5 is the clearest example: the surrogate cross-validation MSE is extremely high, which by a standard model quality benchmark would suggest the approach is failing, but the actual returned outputs have improved steadily from round 3 onwards. Dunbar's framing reminded me to judge the strategy by the outcome it is trying to produce rather than by intermediate diagnostics that were designed for a different purpose.
