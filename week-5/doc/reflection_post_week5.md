# Week 5 Reflection

How did the ideas of hierarchical feature learning influence the way you thought about structuring or refining your optimisation strategy this round?
Learning about hierarchical feature learning made me think more carefully about what the surrogate model might be doing at each layer. In earlier rounds, I think the neural network was mainly picking up on which individual dimensions correlated with higher outputs. With 14 data points now available for the simpler functions, I started to wonder whether the second hidden layer might be capturing interactions between dimensions rather than just individual effects. In F5, dimensions 3 and 4 together seemed to drive larger output jumps than either alone. I am not certain this reflects a genuine interaction at this sample size, but it made me reluctant to reduce the architecture to a single hidden layer.

You saw how breakthroughs such as AlexNet and ImageNet classification reshaped expectations in AI. What parallels do you see between those leaps in performance and the incremental improvements you make in your capstone submissions?
What struck me about AlexNet was that the leap came from combining better architecture with sufficient data at the right moment. I see a loose parallel in F5, where output improved from around 655 in round 3 to 952 in round 4 and a predicted 2628 in round 5 - less like gradual progress and more like a step change once the surrogate had enough data to locate a better region. That said, I am cautious about overstating the comparison. AlexNet generalised across millions of images; my surrogate is working with as few as 14 observations, and the large gap between training and cross validation error for F4 and F5 suggests it may still be fitting noise rather than genuine structure.

When training neural networks, people often weigh trade-offs between depth, complexity and training efficiency. Did you encounter similar trade-offs in deciding whether to explore widely or exploit known promising regions in your queries?
Yes, in a similar form. With a small data set, a more complex surrogate fits training points well but generalises poorly. I kept the network shallow and added an exploration bonus to the acquisition score to avoid pure exploitation. This played out differently across functions: for F8, the query sits 0.81 units from the nearest observed point, suggesting exploration dominated, while for F4 it is only 0.049 away, suggesting the surrogate was confident in a known region. I am not certain which behaviour will prove more useful until the returned outputs arrive.

Reflecting on the building blocks of neural networks (inputs, activations, loss, gradients, weight updates), which of these concepts helped you think differently about how your model learns from the data you've accumulated so far?
Gradients were the most useful concept this round. By computing a numerical gradient of the surrogate output with respect to each input, I could see which dimensions the model was most sensitive to. For F5, gradient magnitudes for dimensions 4 and 3 were around 1027 and 927 respectively, far larger than the others. Looking back at earlier rounds, the queries that improved F5 most were those that pushed precisely those two dimensions higher, so the gradient analysis confirmed something already visible in the data. I also chose tanh over ReLU because it saturates smoothly near the input boundaries, which felt safer for gradient based refinement in that region.

Module 16 also introduced PyTorch and TensorFlow as different frameworks for building and scaling models. If you were to frame your current optimisation approach in terms of a 'framework', would it be closer to rapid prototyping and flexibility or to structured, production-ready design? Why?
My approach is clearly closer to rapid prototyping. The script retrains from scratch each round, key parameters are hardcoded, and no surrogate models are saved between runs. This resembles the run and inspect style of PyTorch rather than a structured pipeline. For now the query budget is the real bottleneck, not compute, so this feels appropriate. If the project continued over more rounds I would need to move toward something more structured with persistent model logging and uncertainty estimates.

In the guest interview, Giovanni Liotta discussed industry applications of deep learning in sport. How might reflecting on real-world deep learning use cases inform the way you benchmark success in your own capstone challenge?
Liotta's point was that model quality should be judged by real outcomes, not validation metrics alone. This applies directly here. The cross validation MSE for F5 is around 117,767, which looks poor in isolation, but what matters is whether the submitted query returns a higher output than round 4 did. The surrogate only needs to be accurate enough to point toward a better region. By that standard, the consistent improvement in F5 (655, 836, 952, predicted 2628) and F8 (predicted 10.2, up from 9.82) suggests the strategy is working, even if the surrogate itself is imperfect.
