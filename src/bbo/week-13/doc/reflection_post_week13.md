# Week 13 Reflection

How has your understanding of the exploration-exploitation trade-off evolved with increasing data? How did you balance taking risks by exploring new strategies versus exploiting those that had proven effective?

My understanding of the trade-off has become more practical and less abstract. At the start, exploration and exploitation felt like two competing goals, but by the final round I saw that the balance depends on the function and on how stable the earlier evidence is. For low-dimensional functions, I could exploit more confidently because the same neighborhoods kept producing strong results. For harder functions, I still needed exploration because the model was more uncertain and the search space remained under-sampled. My final balance was to keep exploiting recurring strong regions while reserving some risk for moving toward new but still structured areas.

As your data set expanded, how did the nature of feedback influence your optimisation process? Can you relate this to how RL agents adjust their reward expectations or update their Q-values?

As the data set grew, feedback became more informative and less noisy, so I trusted it more when it was repeated across rounds rather than when it came from one isolated query. This changed my optimisation process because I started to treat stable patterns as stronger evidence and weak one-off results as less important. That is similar to reinforcement learning, where agents update value estimates after receiving feedback and gradually shift expectations toward better actions. In my case, the surrogate and cluster scores played a role similar to Q-values: they were updated by new evidence and used to guide the next move, but they still had to be tempered by exploration so I did not overfit to early rewards.

Think about the AlphaGo Zero case study. How does your iterative improvement reflect the idea of self-play or autonomous learning? Did your process resemble model-free learning (trial and error) or model-based planning (anticipating future outcomes)?

My process resembles autonomous improvement in the sense that each round builds on the previous round without needing external corrections beyond the challenge feedback. I learn from my own submitted queries, observe the outcome, and then update my strategy for the next round. That is a bit like iterative self-improvement, although it is not self-play in the strict game sense. Overall, my process is closer to model-based planning than pure model-free trial and error because I do not just react to the latest reward; I use a surrogate model, clustering, and distance cues to anticipate which future queries are likely to work better. Even so, there is still a trial-and-error component because the next query is never guaranteed to succeed.

How could RL strategies be applied to enhance exploration design, efficiency or convergence speed in real-world optimisation tasks?

RL strategies could help by learning when to explore and when to exploit instead of using a fixed rule for every stage. For example, an agent could adapt its exploration rate based on uncertainty, recent improvement, or whether a region of the search space is already well covered. That would likely make optimisation more efficient because the algorithm could spend more effort in promising areas and less effort in areas that are already well understood. RL ideas could also improve convergence speed by learning a policy that avoids unproductive random search and instead focuses on actions that historically lead to larger gains. In a real-world setting, this would be useful for tuning models, designing experiments, or controlling systems with limited evaluation budgets.
